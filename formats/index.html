A file format won't fix vague BIM

Open formats are useful. They are not a plan.

Quebec's government BIM roadmap promotes "open BIM": IFC model files, IDS requirement files, the bSDD data dictionary and the other buildingSMART standards. Its guidance document on the subject was written by Érik Poirier, a construction engineering professor, with Cloé Arnould, and approved in May 2025.1 Professor Poirier is one of the most influential voices in Quebec BIM. In 2019 he was already vice-president of the Groupe BIM du Québec and a director of buildingSMART Canada.2

We agree with much of that guidance. We disagree with a reading of it we often hear: that if public owners require IFC and open tools on every project, the industry's BIM problems will go away. In our experience, the main problem in Quebec is not the file format. It is that owners ask for "the BIM" without saying what it is for. A vague model saved as IFC is still a vague model.

The argument in six points

  • A format makes a file readable. It does not say what the file must contain, who must check it, or what it is for.
  • An IFC export is a filtered copy of the model, not a model you can keep designing in. The next team still needs the native files.
  • Where a format was required without clear requirements, the UK's experience with COBie shows the result: costly deliverables that some clients could not use.
  • IFC itself is hard to implement, splits into many dialects, and is about to change its whole approach with IFC 5. buildingSMART says so in its own words.
  • ISO 19650 is a useful shared vocabulary, not a revelation. Much of it renames what Penn State published for free in 2010.
  • Free software lowers the price of licences. The bottleneck is people: owners who can state what they need, and teams trained to deliver and check it.

This is a disagreement about priorities and order, not about anyone's good faith or competence. The guidance itself calls open BIM « naturellement souhaitable » (naturally desirable), and adds that it « demeure la seule solution pérenne et robuste » (remains the only lasting and robust solution) for owners to keep control of their data.1 It sets no obligation or date.

Where we agree

  • Start with the uses. The guide's own summary says « Tout débute avec les cas d’usage » (everything starts with the use cases).1 That is exactly where OpenBEP starts.
  • Owners must control their data. « Il est d’une importance capitale que les DOP conservent le contrôle de leurs données » (it is vital that public owners keep control of their data).1
  • Requirements should be checkable by machine. The guide says that it is « typiquement le client qui développe les IDS » (typically the client who writes the IDS).1 OpenBEP exports an IDS file from the plan for this reason.
  • Honest about IFC's limits. In 2019 Professor Poirier said « L’IFC n’est pas parfait » (IFC is not perfect), and warned, about software not certified by buildingSMART, that « Chaque fois qu’une extraction est effectuée, les données sont filtrées » (every time data is extracted, it is filtered).2 That is right, and it is the heart of our argument.
  • buildingSMART's turn to IDS. buildingSMART saw that a fixed list of IFC subsets could not keep up with what projects need, and called for exchange requirements that can be defined per project and validated by machine.11 IDS, the Information Delivery Specification, answers that need81, and it is the most useful part of open BIM for owners today.
  • The hard part is human. At a public SQI talk in November 2025, answering an audience question as an independent researcher (the question came from OpenBEP's founder), Professor Poirier said the technical side has been settled and that the remaining work is human: bringing large public owners and the industry along together.3 We agree. Our own conclusion goes further: a format requirement is a technical answer to a human problem.

Where we part ways

1. "Open formats guarantee interoperability."

The guide's summary says « Les formats ouverts garantissent l’interopérabilité entre les logiciels » (open formats guarantee interoperability between software).1 A format guarantees that another program can open the file. It does not guarantee that the file holds what the next person needs.

  • The same guide says every IFC export goes through a filter, so the model « sera nécessairement « filtrée » et ne contiendra pas toutes les données que contient la maquette originale » (will necessarily be filtered and will not hold all the data of the original model).1
  • It also says the data must be mapped to the IFC schema through dictionaries and correspondence tables.1 Someone has to build those tables. Without a shared list of what each element must carry, each firm builds its own, project by project.
  • What makes two teams' models work together is an agreement on what each element must contain, at which stage. That agreement is the BIM Execution Plan and its level-of-development table. It works with or without IFC.

2. "IFC keeps the data accessible for the long term."

The guide says open formats like IFC « garantissent que les données resteront accessibles sur le long terme » (guarantee that the data will remain accessible in the long term).1 An archive only keeps what was put into it. If the owner never said which data operations need, an IFC archive keeps a vague model for decades.

  • When Argent, the developer of London's King's Cross, asked its asset managers, facilities managers and tenants what information they actually needed, the answer was quite a short list: spatial data to charge the right rent, and data on maintainable plant for warranties.4 The project manager in charge of its BIM strategy said Full COBie data drops are not the route we want to go down.4

3. "Exchange should run on IFC files."

The guide says the data-centred approach « se fera au moyen de fichiers interopérables, encodés selon le schéma de données IFC » (will be done with interoperable files encoded in the IFC schema).1 The guide also says open BIM lets owners « exporter les données des projets en format IFC et de les migrer vers un autre logiciel » (export project data as IFC and move it to other software).1 IFC is good for showing a model to someone who does not use the same software. It is a poor master copy to migrate from.

  • In 2019 Professor Poirier compared an IFC file to a PDF of a Word file: « Le fichier IFC issu d’un modèle Revit s’apparente au PDF issu d’un fichier Word. On ne peut pas faire du traitement de texte avec un PDF mais on pourra tout de même lire le texte. » (An IFC file from a Revit model is like a PDF from a Word file. You can't do word processing in a PDF, but you can still read the text.)2 His point was that anyone can read an IFC file, where a Revit file cannot. We take the other half of his analogy: you cannot keep working in it.
  • Revit's own help says that opening an IFC file creates a new model from the default template, and points users to linking the IFC as reference information instead.5
  • The Netherlands keeps IFC on the "comply or explain" list that public bodies must require. But in 2019 its Standardisation Forum sharpened the scope: IFC models are suitable only as an output from the source model for communication and coordination, and « ze vervangen niet de bronmodellen » (they do not replace the source models).6

So the native authoring files should be the contractual master. IFC is an extra output, asked for when a named use needs it. This has a cost we accept openly: native files tie the archive to one vendor's format, which is the lock-in the guide warns about.1 Our answer is that an owner keeps control by owning the native files and by stating, use by use, what data it needs and in which format it will receive it. A blanket IFC rule does not give that control on its own.

4. "Owners who require IFC open the market."

In 2019 Professor Poirier said that « les donneurs d’ouvrage qui exigent un format de type IFC […] contribuent à ouvrir le marché » (owners who require an IFC-type format help open the market).2 It may. But requiring a format without saying what goes in it has been tried at scale. The UK government's 2011 construction strategy required COBie, a spreadsheet handover format that is a subset of IFC, on centrally funded projects.48

  • Clients who asked for the format, not the content. Birmingham City University's BIM process manager: When a client just says ‘give me COBie’ without identifying their specific requirements, consultants will be ripping their hair out because that level of information is just not needed to manage buildings. It’s a big job for someone that will have to be paid for unnecessarily.4
  • Data that could not reach the client's systems. Three of the people BIM+ interviewed in 2016, the year the UK mandate took effect, two of them from universities, reported difficulty importing COBie data into their maintenance software. Hampshire County Council's framework manager: We want to be able to transfer data into our CAFM systems after project delivery, but have not delivered that yet. In the same survey, 58% of respondents said they had never used BS 1192-4, the standard that describes COBie.4
  • A copy that is out of date on arrival. A senior BIM engineer wrote that COBie is the BIM equivalent of the two-inch thick phone directory that we all used to receive every year – it's out of date even as it lands on your doorstep, because the spreadsheet is independent of the model and every change has to be synchronised by hand.7
  • Still misunderstood years later. In 2020, nine years after the UK construction strategy, John Ford, a contractor's digital lead and an advocate of IFC and COBie, wrote that the lack of understanding of what COBie is, its purpose and how it is driven by IFC were three of its fundamental barriers today, followed by the lack of native support in design, data-collection and maintenance software. His remedy was more IFC knowledge and IDS.8
  • Contracts that pay for files, not information. The UK government-funded BIM Interoperability Expert Group's 2020 consultation found that every respondent on contracts (100%) agreed that common contracts drive the delivery of documents, not the delivery of usable, accessible, sufficient quality information, and that handover of the physical asset takes priority over complete and validated information about the asset.9

To be fair, the same 2016 article quotes clients who valued full COBie data, such as Sandwell council for its housing, and expected the Government Soft Landings process to improve it.4 The difference is that those clients knew what they would do with the data.

Wales, which has no BIM mandate, shows the same gap from the other side. In a 2026 study, a contractor said: We get EIRs that are either copied from old projects or so generic they're useless. Another participant: So much of the documentation is just done to tick a box. Participants described COBie outputs delivered as static spreadsheets late in delivery, and one summed up what they wanted: We don't need a mandate - we need a map.10

None of these people blamed the idea of open data. They pointed to the same gap: the format was required before the client knew, and wrote down, what it needed.

What did it cost?

We looked for measured figures: hours or dollars lost to COBie or IFC deliverables on a named project. We found none published. The sources above describe the waste in words, not numbers. The closest we have is on the Why OpenBEP page: a UK contractor's digital lead reported clients receiving handover data while still paying a maintenance contractor to collect the same data again. If you know of a measured case, tell us and we will add it with its source.

5. "ISO 19650 is the reference everyone must follow."

ISO 19650 is often presented as if being an international standard made it the one source of truth. It is useful, but most of its core ideas were published years earlier, for free.

  • In 2010, Penn State's Computer Integrated Construction research group published its BIM Project Execution Planning Guide under a Creative Commons licence. Its procedure has four primary steps: 1) identify BIM Goals and BIM Uses, 2) design BIM Project Execution Process, 3) develop Information Exchanges, 4) define supporting infrastructure for BIM Implementation.14
  • ISO 19650-1 and -2 came out in 2018. They replaced British standards which, in the UK guidance's own words, formed the basis of the UK approach to BIM.15 The UK guidance also says plainly: The information content is the same information which has always been required during an asset's lifecycle.16
  • Set side by side, much of it is a renaming. Penn State's BIM goals and uses became the owner's "information requirements". Its information exchanges became "exchange information requirements" and delivery plans. Its BIM Project Execution Plan became the "BIM execution plan". ISO 19650 does add useful things from the British standards, such as the chain of appointments and the workflow of the common data environment.
  • So ISO 19650 is a good shared vocabulary for contracts. It is not a revelation, it does not tell an owner which uses it needs, and its text is sold under licence while Penn State's guide is free. Good practice from Penn State, the US National BIM Standard and owners' own guides is just as valid where it is clearer.

Where the guide promises too much

Some passages of the guide describe what open BIM could be one day as if it were what owners get today.

  • The internet comparison. The guide asks readers to imagine the internet without standards like HTML, HTTP or TCP/IP, where « Vous auriez besoin d’un navigateur différent pour chaque site web que vous visitez » (you would need a different browser for each website).1 The comparison flatters IFC. An IFC file can open without error and still be missing what you need, and in 2019 buildingSMART itself counted almost 20 MVDs, which it called 20 different dialects of IFC, planned for the next release.11
  • "Easy to adopt". The guide's figure of the pillars of open BIM, taken from buildingSMART International, says « Le BIM ouvert est facile à adopter » (open BIM is easy to adopt).1 The evidence above says otherwise: in 2016, the year the UK mandate took effect, 58% of respondents had never used the COBie standard, and only 8 of 39 inspected IFC models had sufficient semantics.412
  • Data usable "decades later". The guide says data created in a design tool « pourra être utilisée dans un logiciel de gestion des actifs des décennies plus tard » (can be used in asset management software decades later).1 That is a promise, not a track record. UK clients reported that they could not yet load COBie into their maintenance systems at handover, let alone decades on.4
  • A standard that follows industry needs. The guide says open BIM « évolue selon les besoins de l’industrie dans son ensemble avec une vision à long terme » (evolves with the needs of the whole industry, with a long-term vision).1 In buildingSMART's own words, staying on the MVD track would mean each new use case needs a formal approval process of several years.11 Projects cannot wait that long.

The standard itself is part of the problem

Requiring IFC on every project means asking every firm to carry the weight of the standard as it is today. buildingSMART, the body that maintains IFC, and the researchers who work with it have been frank about that weight.

Too big to implement, so it splits into dialects.

  • buildingSMART's own blog: This rich set of agreements is often too much for a software developer to implement in full into their software tool. Software therefore implements subsets, called model view definitions (MVDs).11
  • The same article counts almost 20 MVDs in the next release: That means 20 different dialects of IFC. Twenty different implementations in software. When this continues the number of MVDs will reach hundred before we realize it. It admits As a community we have been explaining to outsiders that 'there is no one IFC', and calls the approach not sustainable.11
  • So "deliver an IFC" in a contract means little on its own. Someone has to say which version, which subset and which properties, and check that the receiving software supports them.

The same thing can be written many ways.

  • A 2021 study inspected 57 IFC models produced in practice, almost all in the older IFC2x3 version. The authors found that IFC's flexibility allows several interpretations, which results in heterogeneous IFC solutions for similar situations which significantly hinders interoperability.12
  • Only 8 of the 39 models they checked had sufficient semantics. They also noted that a tool systematically validating IFC geometries is at present missing, and concluded that it would be necessary to act on the simplification of the IFC model.12 buildingSMART now offers a free online IFC validation service, which the Quebec guide describes for public owners checking the files they receive.1

Geometry that needs a cleanup, and files that grow large.

  • buildingSMART's blog says the current resource layer (mainly geometry) needs a cleanup, and that different MVDs can use different geometry types.11
  • Quebec's own guide warns that IFC files « peuvent devenir volumineux et exiger des ressources » (can become large and demand resources), so models must be split into smaller files.1

A moving target.

  • The guide says more than 18 versions of the schema have been published since 1997, and that IFC 5 « adoptera une toute nouvelle approche » (will take a completely new approach).1
  • In October 2026, buildingSMART's IFC 5 repository still describes its examples as preliminary and not suitable for production use, and says Further documentation will follow soon.13
  • Change is slow. buildingSMART's blog warned that the MVD route would need a formal approval process of several years for each use case.11 IFC 4.3 was still a candidate in 20208 and became the ISO standard in 2024.1

Hard to read.

This one is our own view, from daily work: the IFC documentation is very hard to use, even for experienced BIM managers. Finding how one property of one element should be written means going through layers of entities, property sets, MVDs and versions. buildingSMART itself calls MVD one of the most confusing acronyms in its vocabulary.11 A standard that experts struggle to read is a poor base for a requirement placed on every firm in Quebec, large or small.

And the tools

6. "Open-source tools will lower the cost of BIM."

Open-source tools are welcome. The guide notes that several open-source tools can create IDS files, and that is a real help.1 But in our experience, the price of a licence is rarely what stops a project from getting useful BIM. What stops it is an owner who cannot say what it needs, a team that was never trained to deliver it, and nobody checking the result. What the Welsh study's participants asked for was practical templates, worked examples and validation tools; the authors add role-specific training.10 A free tool in untrained hands produces a free vague model.

What owners should ask for first

  1. The uses. Name the BIM uses you need, phase by phase: coordination, quantities, permits, operations. No use, no requirement.
  2. The content. For each use, list the elements, the level of development at each stage, and the parameters each element must carry.
  3. The master files. Make the native authoring files the contractual deliverable, so the next team can keep working.
  4. IFC only where a use needs it. Ask for IFC when a named party must use the model without the authoring software, for example coordination across different tools or a permit portal. Say which version and which properties.
  5. Checkable requirements. Issue the requirements as an IDS file as well, so a delivery can be checked automatically.
  6. Checks with consequences. Check the model at each milestone and hold payment until it passes.
  7. Handover data people will use. Ask operations for the short list of asset data they actually use, in the format their system imports. Don't ask for COBie by default.

OpenBEP does these steps in order. The owner picks the uses, and the BEP Builder turns them into the plan, the element-by-element requirements, the shared parameters, an IDS file and the milestone checks.

What this page does not say

  • It does not say IFC is useless. IFC is the right tool when someone without the authoring software must use the model.
  • It does not say open standards are a bad idea. ISO 19650, IDS and bSDD are worth using.
  • It does not question anyone's motives. The roadmap's authors have been open about its limits, and much of the evidence for those limits comes from their own documents.
